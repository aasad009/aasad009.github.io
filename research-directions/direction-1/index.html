Anatomical test: In real extant species, remove all soft-tissue inputs before reconstruction and compare with independent CT, diceCT or dissection-derived paths and volumes.

Phylogenetic test: Hold out an entire lineage, not random specimens from a training clade. Evaluate anatomical prediction error, interval coverage, and whether constraints add information beyond phylogenetic bracketing.

Full research proposal

Which Muscles Can Skeletal Anatomy Predict?

An extant-calibrated, mechanics-constrained test of how reliably bones predict homologous muscle geometry

Evolutionary question

Skeletal form and muscular anatomy covary, but the relationship is not one-to-one. I want to test how much of a muscle’s location, three-dimensional path, attachment extent and volume can be predicted from fossil-observable skeletal features in living vertebrates. The central output would be a calibrated range of possible anatomies—not a single attractive reconstruction. Understanding those limits is useful for comparative anatomy in living species and can later inform inferences about extinct lineages.

What previous research establishes—and what remains open

This is not the first attempt to reconstruct missing musculature. Bishop and Pierce (2024, 2026) have already established phylogenetically informed attachment and homology reconstructions in synapsids. Demuth et al. (2022) developed and tested three-dimensional polygonal muscle reconstructions against living specimens, with imperfect mass accuracy. More recently, Riem et al. (2026) showed that bone volumes predict individual muscle volumes in a large human MRI cohort. The unresolved question is whether a mechanics-constrained, uncertainty-calibrated relationship transfers across phylogenetically distant living vertebrates and still helps when muscle information is deliberately hidden.

Hypothesis and competing explanations

My hypothesis is that bone geometry, preserved attachment evidence and relatedness can predict some homologous muscle properties with useful, calibrated uncertainty, but that the recoverable information differs substantially by muscle, taxon and evidence quality. I would test whether anatomical and mechanical constraints improve held-out predictions beyond allometry and extant phylogenetic bracketing. A narrow interval only counts as an advance if it actually contains the hidden anatomy at the advertised rate.

• Alternative 1 — The bones are not informative enough. Size, phylogeny and attachment traces leave muscle paths or volumes too uncertain to improve reconstruction.

• Alternative 2 — Existing approaches suffice. Physics-based screening removes obvious intersections but adds no out-of-clade predictive accuracy beyond the methods of Demuth et al. or a careful comparative bracket.

• Alternative 3 — Transfer breaks across clades. Apparent success on randomly held-out specimens disappears when a lineage or distinct locomotor morphology is withheld.

Research plan and decisive tests

Aim 1 | Measure what skeletons predict across living vertebrates.

I would begin with one clearly homologized shoulder–elbow muscle group in a small number of carefully matched living mammal and reptile specimens, subject to access. Bone CT or surface geometry would be paired at the specimen level with dissection, MRI or diceCT measurements of muscle paths, attachment regions and volumes. Before reconstruction, I would hide all tissue information that a fossil would not preserve. The main test is how closely predicted ranges match the withheld three-dimensional anatomy; it does not assume that all muscle types are equally reconstructable.

Aim 2 | Add physically meaningful constraints.

I would compare size-based scaling, extant phylogenetic bracketing, manual volumetric reconstruction and probabilistic anatomical models with and without mechanical constraints. Plausible routes must connect homologous attachments, wrap around bones without penetrating them, and have reasonable length changes over specified joint poses. Mechanical screening can reject impossible paths; it cannot infer activation, fascicle architecture, force capacity or the true muscle route uniquely. I would evaluate both error and interval calibration on whole-species or whole-clade holdouts.

Aim 3 | Test comparative function, then extend to fossil anatomy.

I would quantify which muscle traits are predictable across different living vertebrates, and whether the atlas identifies skeletal observations that would be most useful to collect next. Only if those tests succeed would I transfer uncertainty distributions to well-documented extinct taxa, using Pierce and Bishop’s attachment reconstructions as explicit phylogenetic priors. The historical analysis would report which comparisons survive the uncertainty and which remain unresolved. The deliverable would be a muscle-by-muscle recoverability map, including the traits that cannot be resolved.

Validation and comparisons

• Primary outcomes. Held-out three-dimensional muscle path distance, attachment-region overlap, volume error and empirical coverage of predicted 50% and 90% intervals, all reported separately by muscle and taxon.

• Baselines. Body-size and bone-volume regression; extant phylogenetic bracketing; Demuth-style manually constructed muscle volumes; and a learned anatomical atlas with and without mechanical screening.

• Leakage prevention. Withhold an entire species or clade before fitting; do not allow the hidden dissection, muscle volume or non-osteological attachment information into the predictor. Record which attachment traces could realistically survive fossilization.

• Sample-size and identifiability limits. A few paired specimens can establish a reproducible pilot, not a universally transferable distribution. Measure the effect of phylogenetic coverage and preservation quality; do not use thin confidence bands as evidence of accuracy without held-out coverage.

What would count as evidence

• Success. The full candidate set predicts held-out muscle properties with useful, calibrated uncertainty and improves on simpler comparative baselines in at least one well-defined muscle group.

• Informative failure. If skeletal evidence cannot constrain a property, map that information limit and identify which additional extant measurement would be most informative. This is still a useful comparative-anatomy result.

• Evolutionary interpretation. The resulting distribution is conditional on observed extant taxa, homology assumptions, and preservation. It is not a direct measurement of extinct muscular anatomy or proof that one lineage evolved a particular muscle force.

Feasibility gates and staged milestones

Months 0–3: audit available specimen-matched bone and muscle data, select one homologous muscle group, and specify the holdout taxa, traits and baseline models before examining hidden anatomy. Months 4–9: reproduce an existing reconstruction baseline and evaluate prediction error and interval coverage on the usable pairs. Months 10–18: expand across taxa only if matched specimens and anatomist-reviewed homology support transfer tests. Otherwise, report a reproducible pilot and the exact data gap, without claiming cross-clade validation.

Intellectual merit and complementary expertise

The intellectual merit is a test of which muscle properties are actually encoded in fossil-observable bone and which remain fundamentally ambiguous. A calibrated recoverability map would make evolutionary reconstructions more falsifiable, rather than merely more detailed. Pierce’s group would lead homology, taxon choice and evolutionary interpretation; I would lead inverse-mechanics screening, uncertainty calibration and blinded comparisons. The needed specimen access is not yet assumed.

Broader impacts

Subject to specimen permissions, release a documented, rights-cleared masked-muscle benchmark and scripts for reproducing prediction intervals. An openly accessible teaching module would show why skeletal evidence need not imply one unique anatomy. The initial assessable outputs would be one versioned benchmark and one documented teaching example; any classroom deployment would be arranged with an actual educational partner.
